\paragraph{What to do in practice.} The results reduce to three cases.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item If the merge averages or sparsifies the updates, aligned first or not,
  or is a solver whose matrix is built from data or is diagonal, the
  initialisation does not matter and no action is needed.
\item If the merge solves a least-squares problem whose matrix is built from
  the adapters alone, as adapter-only RegMean, WUDI-Merging and our encoder
  do, run Algorithm~\ref{alg:audit} first. On our own separated cohorts these
  merges work, but on public cohorts adapter-built solvers also fail for
  reasons unrelated to the collapse (\S\ref{subsec:against}), so the merged
  model needs checking in any case. On a collapsed cohort, add an isotropic
  ridge to the closed-form solvers, start near $\lambda = 0.01$ and sweep it on
  held-out data, and do not rely on RegMean's shipped $\alpha$. We did not test
  a repair for WUDI as published; running it ten times longer did not help,
  making its NLL worse on three bases and leaving it within noise on the fourth
  (Table~\ref{tab:wudi}), so on a collapsed cohort we would use one of the
  unaffected merges instead.
\item If several tasks are fine-tuned with Unsloth or LLaMA-Factory at default
  settings, or each from one existing adapter, the adapters are collapsed; so
  is a PiSSA cohort whatever its seeds, and OLoRA and MiLoRA start every task
  from the same $A$ in the same way. A different seed per task avoids the first
  case and not the others. Designs that share $A$ deliberately, such as
  HydraLoRA or federated LoRA with a frozen or shared $A$, are collapsed by
  construction.
\end{itemize}
The audit is worth running even though a ridge repairs most of the damage. A
sweep needs held-out data and one merge per value of $\lambda$, and no
data-free rule we tested sets $\lambda$ well (\S\ref{subsec:lambda}). A user
who has no data for the tasks, or who runs a method at its defaults, meets the
zeros of Table~\ref{tab:wudi} and Figure~\ref{fig:exposure} with no warning.
The audit needs only the $A$ factors and a CPU minute, and it says before any
merge whether the choice of method matters at all.

\paragraph{Predictions that failed.} We collect here the registered
predictions that did not hold, grouped by what they cost. Four changed what
this paper claims. Published WUDI was predicted to be protected by its
early-stopped optimiser, and it is not, in NLL or in accuracy; this is the
lead result (\S\ref{subsec:wudi}). TSV-M was predicted to be exposed and is
not, so the rule is stated for least-squares merges alone
(\S\ref{subsec:results}). The collapse was predicted to be visible on all four
tasks and is invisible on IFEval, where the instruction adapter has little
skill to lose (\S\ref{subsec:beyond}). Solver failures on public cohorts were
predicted to be specific to the collapse and are not (\S\ref{subsec:against}).

Six were explanations that did not survive. The closed form of WUDI does not
concentrate its energy in the ill-conditioned directions, and the 3000-step arm
does not lie between the other two on two of the four bases
(\S\ref{subsec:wudi}). The heuristics' slight lean towards the shared arm did
not vanish at three cohorts per arm, and merging without truncation did not
halve it (\S\ref{subsec:results}). Neither the factorisation freedom of LoRA
nor the spread of adapter norms explains the public failures
(\S\ref{subsec:against}).

Others concern tools for setting the ridge without data, and none of them
works (\S\ref{subsec:lambda}). The rule that targets a condition number was
worse than a fixed $\lambda = 0.01$ on every collapsed cohort of ours, missed
its criterion of doing no harm on two of six separated cohorts (by doing
better than the minimal ridge), and met its criterion on collapsed public
cohorts on two of six. GCV and the L-curve failed all four of their criteria.
A fixed $\lambda = 0.01$, registered as enough on collapsed public cohorts,
was enough on one of six.

Three more concern details. A fourfold scale mismatch between adapters was
predicted not to be specific to solvers, and it multiplies the solver's excess
by $3.9$ and $5.3$ while lowering that of task arithmetic
(\S\ref{subsec:against}). RegMean with activation Grams produced solutions
$2.2$ to $6.5$ times the mean task update rather than at most three; the
amplification is the same on both arms and comes from the activation Grams
themselves, whose condition numbers reach $10^{7}$ whatever the
initialisation. The retrained translation adapter improved but did not become
the best adapter at translation.

\paragraph{What we cannot explain.} Three observations have no tested
explanation. On CLIP ViT-B/32, published WUDI merges shared cohorts
\emph{better} than independent ones, $0.454$ against $0.358$, the opposite of
its behaviour on language models (\S\ref{subsec:beyond}); the description we
give in \S\ref{subsec:wudi}, in which Adam's steps leave the row space on a
collapsed cohort, does not account for it. Scaling the initial $A$ by $0.25$
leaves a cohort collapsed, yet adapter-only RegMean then does less damage than
on the independent arm ($0.022$ against $0.071$ nats), at the same
$\kappa(\bar H)$ as a cohort where it does a great deal (\S\ref{sec:theory}).
That points at the amplification rather than the condition number as what a
solver responds to, but it rests on two cohorts. And the heuristics' lean of
$0.7$ points towards the shared arm, like the solver failures on public
cohorts, survives every explanation we tested.

\paragraph{Relation to the earlier version.} The preprint this paper
supersedes~\citep{pathak2026rd} reported that our closed-form encoder had the
lowest worst-task loss of ten methods on all four bases. That advantage came
from the shared initialisation of its cohorts. In the registered comparison on
independent cohorts the encoder beats the best baseline on one base, ties on
one and loses on two, and it beats it on none once the inference path is
matched and the untrained translation adapter is set aside. The preprint also
reported that worst-task NLL tracks the downstream ranking of methods. With
five or six methods that correlation cannot be measured reliably, and on
HumanEval its sign is wrong, which is why every accuracy claim here is
measured in accuracy.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The controlled experiments use four base models of 7
to 9 billion parameters and four tasks. One adapter did not train properly,
and in accuracy several adapters do not beat the base model on their own
benchmark (on Llama-3.1-8B neither the GSM8K nor the code adapter does), so the
merged models contain weak members. The seed ablation, the task-count and rank
sweep and the out-of-sample test of the scalar law are on one base; the vision
replication is one model; DoRA was measured in geometry only. Larger models,
other architectures and other adapter types are untested. The value of
$\kappa(\bar H)$ depends on the projector curvature model, while the principal
cosines, which carry the audit, are independent of it. The theory is a
leading-order expansion verified numerically, not a bound. The float32 values
we report for the unregularised encoder on shared cohorts are single draws,
which move by $0.02$ to $0.08$ nats between draws (\S\ref{sec:merging}). The
selected ridge $\lambda^\star$ was chosen on the evaluation sets it is scored
on, so the gains we report for a tuned ridge are optimistic; the fixed
$\lambda = 0.01$ is not affected. The tests on translation and IFEval, and
MaTS in accuracy, use one cohort per arm, so their nulls are at the resolution
of the gate. Training and evaluation data overlap on two tasks ($13$ to $16\%$
for instruction following and $9$ to $12\%$ for code), so absolute scores are
optimistic; every comparison here is between the two initialisation arms on
the same data, and on the disjoint subset the ordering of methods is unchanged
in $19$ of $20$ cases. The library reading is of pinned versions, and defaults
can change. The prevalence of $40\%$ is among public cohorts as the sampling
frame defines them, and most of the collapsed ones are versions of one model
rather than tasks one would merge (\S\ref{subsec:public}). Finally,
adapter-built solvers fail often on released adapters for reasons we have not
identified; those failures should not be attributed to the collapse, and such
solvers should not be used on public adapters without checking the result.
