Low-rank adaptation (LoRA)~\citep{hu2022lora} makes it cheap to produce many
specialised versions of one base model, and correspondingly attractive to merge
several of them back into a single set of weights. Many merging methods now
exist: task arithmetic~\citep{ilharco2023task}, sign election
(TIES)~\citep{yadav2023ties}, stochastic sparsification
(DARE)~\citep{yu2024dare}, subspace alignment (KnOTS)~\citep{stoica2025knots},
whitening (TSV-M)~\citep{gargiulo2025tsv}, least-squares solvers in the
RegMean line~\citep{jin2023regmean,tam2024mats}, and data-free methods such as
WUDI-Merging~\citep{cheng2025wudi}, which solves a least-squares problem built
from the task updates themselves. They are compared mostly by benchmark
averages, and the comparisons are unstable: a method that works on one set of
adapters can fail on another, with no way to tell in advance which case
applies.

This paper studies one property of an adapter set that is fixed before
training starts and is almost never reported. LoRA writes each update as $BA$,
starts $B$ at zero and draws $A$ at random. Whether the $T$ adapters of a
cohort drew \emph{one} $A$ or $T$ independent ones is decided by the random
seed in force when the adapters are created. Because $B = 0$ at the start, $A$
moves little during training, about $13\%$ of its norm in our measurements, so
adapters that start from the same $A$ stay close to it. A single global seed,
the natural choice when the seed is meant to make runs reproducible, therefore
gives every adapter of a cohort nearly the same input subspace, whatever its
task.

The consequence we lead with concerns a published method. WUDI-Merging needs
no data, and its authors minimise its objective with an early-stopped
optimiser rather than solving the system, which we expected to act as an
implicit regulariser and registered as protective. It is not. Run exactly as
its authors run it, WUDI leaves a worst-task loss excess $4.7$ to $47.6$ times
larger on cohorts with a shared $A$ than on cohorts with independent ones, on
all four base models we tested. On GSM8K the shared cohorts score $0.034$
against $0.695$ on Llama-3.1-8B, and on HumanEval they score zero on two of the
four bases. We had written in advance that this outcome, if it occurred, would
become the lead result. It belongs to a class that we can name precisely. On
language models, a shared $A$ breaks every merge we tested that solves a
least-squares problem, in closed form or by gradient descent, whose matrix is
built from the adapters alone, and no other.

We make four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{The collapse, measured.} On four base models with 7 to 9 billion
parameters, sharing the initial $A$ moves the median principal cosine between
task subspaces from about $0.05$ to above $0.99$. A registered ablation shows
that the seed in force when the adapters are created decides the regime by
itself, and the data order and dropout do not. The collapse holds at every
task count and rank we tested and under every one of seven changes to
training. It also arises without a shared seed, from PiSSA
initialisation~\citep{meng2024pissa} and from training every task on from one
existing adapter, and it holds for DoRA and for adapters on CLIP ViT-B/32
(\S\ref{sec:collapse}).
\item \textbf{A first-order theory of its conditioning.} Treating each
adapter's $A$ as a small displacement of the shared start, we derive that the
condition number of the operator a solver inverts grows as the inverse square
of the displacement, and the norm of the exact interpolator as its inverse. The
predicted condition number matches the exact value within $0.10\%$ in all $21$
configurations we measured. A scalar law derived from it, fixed on four
cohorts, predicts the condition number of eight cohorts trained afterwards,
seven of them under changed training, within a factor of $2$ on all eight
(\S\ref{sec:theory}).
\item \textbf{Where it occurs.} At their default settings, Unsloth and
LLaMA-Factory seed the random generator before creating the adapter, so every
run on a base model with the same target modules and rank receives the same
initial $A$; we read this in their source and measured it. An audit that reads
only the $A$ factors finds the regime in about a CPU minute, with no data and
no forward pass. Under a sampling frame fixed before any download, it finds
$18$ of $45$ public LoRA cohorts on HuggingFace collapsed, among them a cohort
released with a merging paper (\S\ref{sec:prevalence}).
\item \textbf{Which merges it affects.} We tested twelve merging methods, ten
of them published methods we did not build, and compared each with itself
across the two regimes in NLL and in accuracy. Averaging and sparsifying
heuristics are equivalent across the regimes within $3$ accuracy points on
GSM8K and $5$ on HumanEval. Solvers whose matrix is built from data (RegMean
with activation Grams, MaTS) or is diagonal (Fisher merging) are unaffected,
and whitening (TSV-M), which we predicted would be exposed, is not. Three
solvers built from the adapters fail: published WUDI, a data-free form of
RegMean that scores zero on both benchmarks on Mistral-7B, and the closed-form
encoder of our earlier work. A ridge recovers most of the loss on three of
four bases, RegMean's shipped regulariser is too weak to, and recomputing the
solvers in float64 shows that rounding error is not the cause. On CLIP
ViT-B/32 every method we ran behaves as on language models except published
WUDI,
which merges shared cohorts better there, an outcome we cannot explain
(\S\ref{sec:merging}).
\end{enumerate}

A practitioner who merges with an averaging, sparsifying or data-built method
loses nothing to a shared initialisation. One who merges with a solver built
from the adapters alone should run the audit first, because a ridge cannot be
set without held-out data, and on a collapsed cohort should either regularise
and sweep on such data or use an unaffected merge. One who fine-tunes several
tasks with Unsloth or LLaMA-Factory at default settings should know that the
adapters are collapsed.

\paragraph{How the evidence was produced.} Every confirmatory test in this
paper was pre-registered: the prediction, the decision rule and the
consequence of each outcome were committed to version control before the
compute they govern was run. The repository is public (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}),
and the ordering can be checked with \texttt{git} (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}).
The collapse itself was found after the fact, in an audit of the code behind
an earlier version of this work, organised around a rate-distortion bound and
public as a preprint~\citep{pathak2026rd}; every test of it since was
registered first. Analyses we added after seeing a result are labelled as such
where they appear. Many predictions failed, among them the one that WUDI's
optimiser would protect it; each is reported in its section, and
\S\ref{sec:discussion} collects them and notes the two claims of the earlier
version that this paper does not repeat.
