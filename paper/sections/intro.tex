Low-rank adaptation~\citep{hu2022lora} has made it cheap to produce many
specialised versions of one base model, and correspondingly attractive to
fold several of them back into a single set of weights. A large family of
merging heuristics now exists for this: task
arithmetic~\citep{ilharco2023task}, sign-election methods such as
TIES~\citep{yadav2023ties}, stochastic sparsification such as
DARE~\citep{yu2024dare}, subspace alignment such as
KnOTS~\citep{stoica2025knots}, and closed-form least-squares approaches in
the RegMean line~\citep{jin2023regmean,lorm2024,regmeanpp2025}.

These methods are evaluated almost entirely by benchmark averages, and
the resulting picture is unstable. The same method can be best on one
cohort of adapters and worst on another, and practitioners have no way to
tell in advance which situation they are in. We think a large part of this
instability is not a property of the algorithms at all. It is a property
of the adapters being merged, and it is measurable before any merge is
attempted.

\paragraph{A rate-distortion view.} We treat merging as lossy compression.
$T$ task vectors must be summarised by one weight vector, and the figure
of merit is the \emph{worst-task} excess loss, because a merged model that
collapses on a single task is not deployable. In
\S\ref{sec:lower_bound} we prove a lower bound on this quantity that holds
for every merging algorithm, including ones that see the full curvature
data for free. The bound splits into two terms with very different
characters:
\begin{equation*}
  \underbrace{B^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{\deff}{Tr}\Bigr)}_{\text{irreducible floor}}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{c\cdot\frac{B^2\deff}{Tr}\,2^{-2R/\deff}}_{\text{rate term}} .
\end{equation*}
The second term is the familiar compression cost and vanishes as the rate
grows. The first does not. It is an irreducible floor that no algorithm,
at any rate, can go below, and it is controlled entirely by one geometric
quantity: the effective dimension $\deff$ of the union of the $T$ task
subspaces. When the task subspaces are near-orthogonal, $\deff = Tr$ and
the floor is exactly zero, so every nat of observed merging loss is
algorithmic slack that better methods could in principle recover. When the
subspaces overlap heavily, $\deff \ll Tr$ and most of the observed loss is
information-theoretic and permanently unrecoverable.

\paragraph{The regime is decided by a training detail.} Our main empirical
contribution is that this distinction is not hypothetical, and that real
LoRA cohorts land on either side of it for a reason nobody reports. In the
standard implementation the LoRA $A$ factor is drawn from a single global
seed and $B$ is initialised to zero, so if all $T$ adapters in a cohort are
trained from the same seed they all start from the \emph{same} $A$. Because
$B$ starts at zero, $A$ moves very little during training, and the $T$
task subspaces end up nearly collinear.

We measure this directly on four base models, comparing a shared-seed
cohort against three independently seeded ones
(\S\ref{sec:regimes}, Figure~\ref{fig:two-regimes}). The shared cohort has
median principal cosine $0.996$ between task subspaces, soft $\deff$ of
$16.3$ out of a possible $64$, and an irreducible floor of $0.745B^2$.
The independently seeded cohorts have cosines of $0.047$ to $0.050$, soft
$\deff$ of $63.1$ to $63.3$, and a floor of $0.012B^2$, lower by a factor
of about $60$. Measured with a hard rank tolerance the gap is starker: all
twelve independent cells hold $\deff = Tr = 64$ at every tolerance from
$10^{-6}$ to $10^{-1}$, whereas the shared cohort's effective dimension
falls to $20.8$ once the tolerance is realistic. The three independent
cohorts agree with each other to three decimal places, so this is a
structural property and not a seed accident.

The measurement needs only the adapter weights. It runs in about one CPU
minute, requires no forward passes and no evaluation data, and it tells
you whether the cohort you are about to merge has recoverable headroom at
all.

\paragraph{What we tested, and what did not survive.} A theory that
predicts headroom invites the obvious follow-up: does a rate-distortion
encoder built to exploit that headroom actually win? We built one, and on
the shared-initialisation cohorts it wins comfortably. To find out whether
that result was real or an artifact of the degenerate geometry, we
pre-registered a replication on the independently initialised cohorts,
committing the decision rules to version control before the compute ran.

It did not survive. On properly initialised cohorts our encoder's
advantage disappears and TIES wins on three of four base models
(\S\ref{sec:methods}). A second pre-registered control, which we wrote
ourselves and which was designed to catch exactly this, forced us to
withdraw a headline claim that published merging benchmarks are confounded
by shared-initialisation geometry: the control showed our own
champion-selection was unstable across cohorts, so we cannot support the
stronger claim about the field. The predicted finite-rate exponent is
falsified on real adapters, where the distortion curve is flat from two
bits upward instead of decaying at the predicted rate. We report all of
these, and we report the pre-registrations verbatim in
Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A closed-form lower bound on worst-task NLL excess for rank-$r$
  merging (Theorem~\ref{thm:general}), valid for arbitrary task-dependent
  curvature and against decoders given the curvature for free, together
  with a matching achievability construction (Theorem~\ref{thm:achv}).
\item The identification of $\deff$ as the quantity that separates
  merging problems with recoverable headroom from those without, and a
  closed form for the resulting floor.
\item A measurement showing that LoRA cohort initialisation moves this
  floor by a factor of about $60$, replicated across three independent
  cohorts and four base models, together with a one-CPU-minute audit that
  reports the regime before merging.
\item A pre-registered evaluation, reported in whichever direction it
  fell, establishing that our own encoder's advantage does not survive on
  properly initialised cohorts, that the predicted rate exponent is
  falsified on real adapters, and that a claim we previously made about
  benchmark confounding must be withdrawn.
\item A negative result for DARE composed with task arithmetic, with a
  mechanism that predicts its own functional form, and a pre-registered
  demonstration that this negative result does \emph{not} extend to DARE
  composed with TIES.
\end{enumerate}
