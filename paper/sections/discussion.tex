\paragraph{What we think survives.} Three things. The lower bound and its
matching construction, which are mathematics and do not depend on any of
the empirical outcomes above. The identification of $\deff$ as the
quantity separating merging problems with recoverable headroom from those
without, together with the closed form for the floor. And the measurement
that LoRA cohort initialisation moves that floor by a factor of about
$60$, replicated across three independent cohorts and four base models,
with a one-CPU-minute audit that reports the regime before any merge.

\paragraph{What does not.} Our own encoder is not the best method on
properly initialised cohorts; TIES is, on three of four base models. The
predicted finite-rate exponent is falsified on real adapters. And the
claim that published merging benchmarks are confounded by
shared-initialisation geometry, which would have been the most
consequential thing here, was withdrawn by a control we wrote ourselves.
We have left all three in the paper at full strength rather than
retreating to the subset that flatters the method, because the regime
result is only worth anything if the reader can trust that we would have
reported it the other way.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The downstream evaluation cannot measure what we wanted it
  to.} We had hoped to show that worst-task NLL excess predicts
  downstream accuracy. It does not, and more importantly our design cannot
  determine whether it does. The Spearman correlations are computed over
  the five or six methods in each row, and under the null
  $\mathrm{SD}(\rho) = 1/\sqrt{n-1}$, which is $0.45$ at $n=6$. Every
  correlation we can compute, in either direction, is within about one and
  a half standard deviations of zero. Values we previously reported as
  evidence were never evidence. Settling this needs roughly $25$ or more
  points per base, obtained by sweeping $\alpha$, $T$, method and seed,
  not a different reading of $n=6$.
\item \textbf{The NLL proxy is very flat.} A zero-mean perturbation that
  changes the merged delta by $37$ to $57\%$ in Frobenius norm costs about
  $0.001$ nats, well under $1\%$ of typical worst-task excess. The metric
  is far less sensitive to the merged weights than one would like, which
  is an independent reason to be cautious about small differences between
  methods.
\item \textbf{Our KnOTS baseline was a no-op, and we have not established
  whose fault that is.} With the default inner combination our
  implementation reduces algebraically to task arithmetic, because
  $V V^\top = I$ cancels, and it matched task arithmetic to four decimals.
  With a working inner merge it separates from task arithmetic on all four
  base models. We report this as an erratum about our own baseline. We
  have not checked the reference implementation, so we do not claim
  anything about published KnOTS.
\item \textbf{The rate-exponent falsification rests on one seed.} See
  \S\ref{subsec:w3}.
\item \textbf{Train and evaluation splits overlap.} Roughly $14.5\%$ of
  evaluation examples on the instruction-following task and $10\%$ on the
  code task also appear in training, because training and evaluation
  shuffle with different seeds over the same pool. This inflates absolute
  numbers. It does not favour any method over another, since all methods
  are evaluated on identical data, so we do not think it affects the
  comparisons, but the absolute values should not be read as held-out
  performance.
\item \textbf{Scope.} Everything here is $T=4$ tasks at rank $r=16$ on
  four base models in the 7B to 9B range, with worst-task NLL excess as
  the metric. We do not know how the regime distinction behaves at much
  larger $T$, where $Tr$ approaches $d$ and $\deff = Tr$ becomes
  impossible.
\item \textbf{One pre-registered control has a known specification
  defect}, described in \S\ref{subsec:withdrawn}, which we did not act on
  because acting on it after seeing the result is what pre-registration
  exists to prevent.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Implications for practice.} The immediate one is free. If you
are training a cohort of LoRA adapters that you intend to merge, draw the
initialisation independently per task rather than from one global seed.
It costs nothing, and on our measurements it is the difference between an
irreducible floor of $0.745B^2$ and one of $0.012B^2$. The second is for
anyone benchmarking merging methods: report the cohort's $\deff$ alongside
the results, because a comparison run on a collinear cohort is measuring
differences against a floor rather than against zero, and report more than
one initialisation draw, because we found single-draw rankings are not
reproducible.

\paragraph{Future work.} The margin-aware version of the stability control
is the obvious next pre-registration, and it may well reinstate the claim
we withdrew. Establishing whether the reference KnOTS implementation has
the same degeneracy ours did would settle whether that finding is about
the field or only about our code. And the base-level moderator visible on
Llama, which is the odd model out in two independent pre-registered tests,
is worth a designed experiment.
