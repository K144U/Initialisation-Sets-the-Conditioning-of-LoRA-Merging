Merging several LoRA adapters into one model sometimes costs almost
nothing and sometimes destroys a task outright, and practitioners have
no cheap way to tell which case they are in before they merge. We give
a closed-form lower bound on worst-task negative log-likelihood excess
for rank-$r$ merging, together with a matching achievability
construction. The bound is governed by a single geometric quantity, the
effective dimension $\deff$ of the union of the task subspaces, and it
takes the form $B^2\bigl(1 - \deff/(Tr)\bigr)$: an irreducible floor
that is large when the task subspaces overlap and vanishes exactly when
they are near-orthogonal.

Our main empirical finding is that this regime distinction is not a
technicality, and that which regime a LoRA cohort occupies is decided
by a training detail that is almost never reported. Across four base
models and three independently drawn cohorts we measure $\deff$
directly. When all adapters in a cohort share one initialisation of the
LoRA $A$ factor, the task subspaces are nearly collinear (median
principal cosine $0.996$), $\deff$ is $16.3$ of a possible $64$, and
the floor sits at $0.745B^2$: about three quarters of the worst-task
loss such a cohort can suffer is information-theoretically
irreducible, and no merging algorithm can remove it. When the adapters
are initialised independently, the same measurement gives cosines of
$0.047$ to $0.050$, $\deff$ of $63.1$ to $63.3$, and a floor of
$0.012B^2$, lower by a factor of about $60$. Measured at a realistic
rank tolerance the separation is sharper still: the shared cohort's
effective dimension falls to $20.8$ of $64$, while the independently
initialised cohorts hold $\deff = Tr = 64$ across four decades of
tolerance. No standard merging diagnostic distinguishes the two
cohorts, and the measurement costs about one CPU-minute with no
forward passes.

We then use pre-registration to test what this predicts about merging
methods, and report the outcome whichever way it fell. Our own
rate-distortion encoder, which wins on the shared-initialisation
cohorts that our earlier draft measured, does not survive the move to
independently initialised ones: TIES wins on three of four base models.
A pre-registered control that we wrote ourselves withdrew our headline
claim that published benchmarks are confounded by shared-initialisation
geometry. The predicted finite-rate exponent is falsified on real
adapters, where the curve is flat from two bits upward rather than
decaying. We report a clean negative result for DARE composed with task
arithmetic, with a mechanism that predicts its own functional form, and
show that the negative result does not extend to DARE composed with
TIES. What survives is the bound, the regime diagnosis, and the audit.
