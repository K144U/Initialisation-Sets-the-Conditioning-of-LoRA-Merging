Merging several LoRA fine-tunes into a single deployable model is now
routine practice, but it is judged purely empirically: methods are
compared on benchmarks with no notion of how well merging could
possibly do. We supply the missing reference point. Casting merging as
lossy compression of $T$ task-specific weight updates into one set of
weights -- judged by the \emph{worst}-affected task, since a merged
model that collapses on one task is not deployable -- we prove the
first rate-distortion theorem for model merging. The theorem splits
worst-task error into two parts with different cures: an
\emph{irreducible floor}, determined entirely by how much the tasks'
weight subspaces overlap and immune to any amount of storage, plus a
\emph{compression term} that decays exponentially in the bit budget.
An explicit encoder (randomized orthogonal mixing followed by uniform
scalar quantization) matches the bound up to a constant factor whose
measured growth in $T$ is logarithmic.

The theorem makes a measurable claim about practice, and we test it.
Across $16$ LoRA adapters ($4$ base models $\times$ $4$ tasks), task
subspaces never overlap: the floor is exactly zero in every layer of
every model. Consequently, everything current methods lose -- a
measured $0.10$--$0.22$ nats/token of worst-task error, with $95\%$
bootstrap confidence intervals -- is \emph{algorithmic slack, not an
information-theoretic limit}. The zero-overlap prediction also
explains method behavior: subspace alignment (KnOTS) cannot and does
not beat naive averaging, sign-conflict resolution (TIES) does, and a
robust ``less-is-more'' anomaly appears in which $2$-bit quantized
merging beats higher-bit merging on all four architectures. The
message for practice: merging quality today is limited by algorithms,
not by information -- and the bound quantifies exactly how much room
is left.
