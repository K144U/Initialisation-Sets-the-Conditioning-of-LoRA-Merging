Merging several LoRA adapters into one model sometimes costs almost nothing
and sometimes destroys a task, and practitioners have had no cheap way to
tell which. One CPU minute on the adapter weights, with no forward passes
and no evaluation data, predicts it. The audit asks whether the cohort's
task subspaces have collapsed onto each other, which is what happens when
the adapters share one random draw of the LoRA $A$ factor: a detail almost
never reported, and one a single global training seed silently decides. It
moves the median principal cosine between task subspaces from $0.047$ to
$0.995$, and under a sampling frame fixed before any download it holds for
$37.8\%$ of $45$ public cohorts. We pre-registered what follows and report
both halves. Collapse is invisible to the heuristics most people use, which
return bounded-norm near-mean solutions: fifteen of twenty base-by-method
cells show no effect at our $0.01$ nat resolution. It is decisive for
methods that solve the linear system, which run $2.1$ to $65$ times worse on
a collapsed cohort until one Tikhonov term repairs them, on two solvers of
which one is not ours. And it reaches deployed behaviour: on Mistral the
unregularised merge scores zero on both GSM8K and HumanEval, every
generation scored and none discarded, and the same ridge recovers it. The
route here was a rate-distortion lower bound, which we prove and report as
provenance rather than as support. Several of our own earlier claims did not
survive pre-registered replication, and we report all of them.
