\paragraph{What we think survives.} The bound and its matching construction,
which are mathematics and do not depend on any empirical outcome here. The
observation that the bound's floor term is \emph{vacuous} for LoRA cohorts in
general position, together with the exact instance form
$B^2 - \tauH^\top\bar H\tauH$ that should be evaluated in its place. The
identification of $\kappa(\bar H)$ as the quantity cohort initialisation
actually controls, measured across four base models and four cohorts, with an
amplification that returns the orthogonal-subspace value $T$ to within
$1.4\%$ at two values of $T$. The pre-registered finding that this conditioning
is invisible to standard merging heuristics and decisive for solve-based ones,
which holds on a second solver we did not design and survives removing the rank
truncation, where the effect is larger rather than smaller. And the measurement
behind it: the heuristics stay within a factor of two of the mean task delta
with essentially none of their energy in the ill-conditioned directions, while
the unregularised solvers reach $35$ to $57$ times with three quarters of
theirs there.

\paragraph{What does not.} Our own encoder. Three controls have each removed
a piece of its advantage: moving to properly initialised cohorts, matching the
rank and the inference path to the baselines, and dropping the adapter that
never trained. After all three it beats the best baseline on no base model at
all, and is worse than it on one. The middle control moved a verdict in our
favour and the other two moved verdicts against us; we report all three the
same way. The claim that published merging benchmarks are
confounded by initialisation geometry was withdrawn by a control we wrote
ourselves. Our earlier report that the finite-rate exponent is falsified is
withdrawn: its two supporting slopes were fitted against an asymptote computed
at a different ridge value, and against matched asymptotes the question is
unresolved rather than settled either way. And worst-task NLL excess, the
metric everything here is measured in, does not predict downstream accuracy on
either benchmark we can test, and inverts on one of them. We have left all of these at full strength rather than retreating
to the subset that flatters the method, because the conditioning result is
only worth anything if the reader can believe we would have reported it the
other way.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The metric's resolution is about $0.001$ nats, and several of
  our differences are near it.} A zero-mean perturbation changing the merged
  delta by $37$ to $57\%$ in Frobenius norm costs roughly that much. Any
  comparison in this paper at the $0.005$ threshold should be read with that
  in mind; it is why we decline to call two of the four bases in
  Table~\ref{tab:q1} losses. The conditioning results are not affected: the
  shared-versus-independent difference at $\lambda = 0$, which is the quantity
  \S\ref{subsec:ridge} turns on, runs from $0.13$ to $9.63$ nats on the
  single-cohort table and $0.22$ to $11.56$ at $n = 3$ per arm
  (\S\ref{subsec:shared-arm}), one to three orders of magnitude above the
  resolution either way.
\item \textbf{Worst-task NLL excess does not predict downstream accuracy,
  and on one benchmark it predicts it backwards.} An earlier version said
  only that we could not measure this, because the Spearman correlations
  were computed over the five or six methods in a single row, where
  $\mathrm{SD}(\rho) = 1/\sqrt{n-1} = 0.45$. That was too weak a statement:
  the correlation does not have to be computed within a row. Pooling over
  the $12$ base-by-cohort blocks that already exist gives a standard error
  of about $0.17$ instead, and the answer is then visible. On grade-school
  mathematics there is no relationship, $\rho = -0.14$ with a
  block-bootstrap interval of $-0.46$ to $+0.21$. On code generation
  $\rho = +0.45$, interval $+0.10$ to $+0.73$, and the sign is the wrong
  one: \emph{higher} NLL excess goes with a \emph{higher} pass rate. It
  survives dropping the one arm with a rank confound ($\rho = +0.48$).

  There is a reading of this that is not damning, and we give it without
  leaning on it: worst-task excess is a maximum over four tasks, so it
  penalises exactly those merges that sacrifice one task to preserve
  another, and a merge that protects the code adapter at the expense of
  translation will look bad by our metric and good on a code benchmark.
  That is a coherent story, and it is also an argument that the metric
  answers a different question from the one a practitioner asks about a
  single deployed capability.

  \textbf{The scope of this limitation is narrower than it was.} What fails
  above is the ranking of merge \emph{methods} within a cohort, where the
  differences are hundredths of a nat. The paper's own effect is not of that
  size, and \S\ref{subsec:downstream-cond} tests it directly: the
  conditioning collapse reaches GSM8K and HumanEval accuracy on four of four
  bases, and on Mistral the unregularised shared-cohort merge scores zero on
  both benchmarks with no scorer discards, recovering to $0.462$ and $0.244$
  with one ridge term. So a claim about method rankings in this unit should
  still not be read as a claim about accuracy. A claim about the
  conditioning regime may be, and that is the claim this paper makes.
\item \textbf{The finite-rate exponent is unmeasured, not merely
  unresolved.} Against matched asymptotes one base fits at $-2.10$
  ($R^2 = 0.98$) and one at $-3.50$, but neither fit is supported at the
  precision this metric provides: the point that makes the first fit
  possible sits $0.0000029$ nats above its asymptote, some $350$ times
  below the metric's own resolution, and two of the second's three points
  are also below it. Ten of twenty-four measured values fall below their
  own asymptote, which the model forbids. We withdraw the reading that the
  exponent holds on any base (\S\ref{subsec:w3}); the sweep lacks the
  dynamic range to measure it, since only $b \in \{1,2\}$ clears the noise
  floor and $b=1$ is excluded as clipping-dominated. A finer low-rate grid
  would settle it; we deliberately have not chosen one after seeing which
  would produce a fit. \emph{No claim in this paper depends on the rate
  term.}
\item \textbf{Our KnOTS baseline was a no-op, and the repair works.} With
  the default inner combination it reduces algebraically to task arithmetic
  and matched it to four decimals; \S\ref{subsec:ridge} measures it at
  amplification $0.99$ with tail fraction $0.000$, identical to task
  arithmetic in both columns. We report that as an erratum about our own
  configuration and claim nothing about published KnOTS. Re-run with a TIES
  inner combination it becomes a genuinely different method, and a good one:
  it beats task arithmetic on all four bases in both cohorts, by $0.020$ to
  $0.080$ nats, and at $T = 3$ on three of four, Llama's margin falling
  inside the tie threshold. So a subspace-alignment method \emph{is}
  represented in our comparison after all. It does not reach the champion
  baseline on any base at either $T$, ranking third or fourth of six
  throughout, so no verdict in Table~\ref{tab:q1} moves.
\item \textbf{One adapter in four never learned its task}, on every base and
  in both arms, so our cohorts are $T=4$ nominal and $3$ effective
  (\S\ref{subsec:scope}). Every geometric quantity is reported at both $T$
  and the contrast is unchanged. The merge results are now reported at both
  $T$ as well, and there one verdict does move: our encoder's win on Llama
  becomes a tie once the untrained adapter is removed (\S\ref{subsec:q1}),
  so its remaining advantage is one of the things this defect was
  supporting. No claim about merging $T$ well-trained tasks is supported by
  this data.
\item \textbf{The ridge sweep's gate was one-sided in provenance, and no
  longer is.} An earlier version could not compute the pre-registered
  $2\times\mathrm{SE}$ gate at all, because there was one cohort per arm;
  a later one had three cohorts on the independent side and one on the
  shared side, so the standard error came entirely from the independent
  arm. Both arms now have three (\S\ref{subsec:shared-arm}). We leave this
  entry in the list rather than deleting it, because the two earlier states
  were published and a reader tracking what changed should find it here.
\item \textbf{Train and evaluation splits overlap} by $12.9$ to $16.2\%$ on
  the instruction task and $9.2$ to $11.6\%$ on the code task, because the
  two draws shuffle one pool with different seeds. Absolute numbers are
  optimistic and no number here is a held-out number. Whether that affects
  the \emph{comparisons} is no longer a matter of belief: re-scoring every
  cell on the disjoint subset leaves the method ordering unchanged in $19$
  of $20$ rows, and the one change swaps two variants of our own encoder
  (Reproducibility Statement).
\item \textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:general}'s rate term is conditional.} It holds
  under Assumption~\ref{asm:epr}, which we state rather than prove: that the
  entropy-power-to-variance ratio of $\eta$ is bounded below uniformly in
  $(T, r, d)$ and in the spectra. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} isolates the single
  step that uses it. The floor term carries no such condition.
\item \textbf{Scope.} $T=4$, $r=16$, four base models in the 7B to 9B range,
  language only, worst-task NLL excess. $Tr = 64$ is far below $d$, so the
  regime where $Tr$ approaches $d$, and where $\deff = Tr$ must fail, is
  untouched. No rank sweep and no $T$ sweep on real adapters.
\item \textbf{One pre-registered control has a known specification defect}
  (\S\ref{subsec:withdrawn}), which we did not act on, because acting on it
  after seeing which way it fell is what pre-registration exists to prevent.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Implications for practice.} The recommendation is conditional,
and it is about worst-task NLL excess, which limitation~2 says does not
predict downstream accuracy. We state both halves rather than the
convenient one.

If you merge with a method that solves a linear system, audit your cohort's
conditioning and regularise. Unregularised, the same merge is $65$ times
worse on an ill-conditioned cohort at rank $16$ and $117$ times without
truncation, on two solvers, one of which we did not build, and one ridge
term removes the difference. This is the recommendation we are most
confident in, because it is the one with a measured mechanism
(Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}) behind it.

If you merge with task arithmetic, TIES, DARE or a similar heuristic, we
find no effect of cohort conditioning larger than about $0.01$ nats, which
is what our design can detect. The exception is subspace alignment, which
gains $0.010$ to $0.013$ nats from independent initialisation on three of
four bases, small but consistent.

For anyone benchmarking merging methods: report $\kappa(\bar H)$ and the
principal cosines alongside the results, since they cost a CPU minute and
change how a solve-based result should be read; report more than one
initialisation draw, because single-draw rankings are not reproducible; and
do not use worst-task NLL excess as a proxy for deployed capability, on our
evidence, since on one of the two benchmarks we tested it ranks methods in
the wrong order.

\paragraph{Future work.} A margin-aware version of the stability control is
the obvious next pre-registration and may reinstate the claim we withdrew. A
finer low-rate grid would settle the exponent. Checking whether the reference
KnOTS implementation shares the degeneracy ours did would establish whether
that finding is about the field or only about our code. And the regime where
$Tr$ approaches $d$, where the floor must stop being vacuous, is where this
bound would start to have operational content; reaching it needs either many
more tasks or much higher rank.
