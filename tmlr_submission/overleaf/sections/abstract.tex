Merging several LoRA adapters into one model sometimes costs almost
nothing and sometimes destroys a task, with no cheap way to tell which in
advance. We show that one detail of how the adapters were trained largely
decides it, and that the detail can be read off the adapter files in about
a minute of CPU time, without running the model. LoRA writes each weight
update as a product $BA$ and initialises $A$ at random, and whether the
$T$ adapters of a cohort drew one $A$ or $T$ independent ones is rarely
reported: a single global training seed silently decides it. Sharing the
draw leaves the tasks in nearly the same subspace, moving the median
principal cosine across four base models from $0.05$ to $0.99$. Under a
sampling frame fixed before any download, $17$ of $45$ public cohorts are
in that state. The consequences split by method. Merges that average or
sparsify task vectors are unaffected: fifteen of twenty base-by-method
cells show no effect on our loss metric, and none of forty show one on
GSM8K or HumanEval. Merges that solve a linear system,
ours and RegMean, run $2$ to $65$ times worse on a collapsed cohort, and
on Mistral the merged model scores zero on GSM8K and on HumanEval; one
Tikhonov term recovers both. So the advice is to measure the cosines
before choosing a merging method. These tests were pre-registered. We
reached them from a rate-distortion lower bound on merging, proved here
modulo one imported constant and one assumption we state but do not
establish; it is what pointed us at the operator the cosines measure,
and we report it as provenance rather than as a contribution.
