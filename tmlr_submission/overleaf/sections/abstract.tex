Merging several LoRA adapters into one model sometimes costs almost
nothing and sometimes destroys a task, with no cheap way to tell which in
advance. We show that one detail of how the adapters were trained largely
decides it, and that the detail can be read off the adapter files in about
a minute of CPU time, without running the model. LoRA starts each adapter
from a random matrix, and whether the $T$ adapters of a cohort started from
one such matrix or from $T$ independent ones is rarely reported: a single
global training seed silently decides it. Sharing it leaves the tasks in
nearly the same subspace, moving the median principal cosine across four
base models from $0.05$ to $0.99$. Under a sampling frame fixed before any
download, $17$ of $45$ public cohorts are in that state. The consequences
split by method. Merges that average or sparsify task vectors are
unaffected: fifteen of twenty base-by-method cells show no effect on our
loss metric, and none of forty show one on GSM8K or HumanEval. Merges that
solve a linear system, ours and RegMean, run $2$ to $65$ times worse on a
collapsed cohort, and on Mistral the merged model scores zero on GSM8K and
on HumanEval; one Tikhonov term recovers both. Regularising such a solve is
standard practice, but RegMean's shipped default is about $20$ times too
small on a collapsed cohort and applies the same dose either way: the
default does not know which regime it is in, and the audit is what tells
you. All of these tests were pre-registered, and several of our own earlier
claims did not survive them.
